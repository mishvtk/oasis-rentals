Flagler County Landfill Appeal: What Renters Should Watch

The proposed 610-acre Project Butterfly landfill has received one local land-use approval, but it remains contested and is not authorized to begin construction or accept waste. Flagler County renters should investigate possible truck traffic, noise, odor, drainage and neighborhood effects without treating them as certain outcomes.

On September 14, 2026, Flagler County commissioners agreed unanimously to challenge the Bunnell planning board’s approval. The appeal adds another public decision before the proposal could move into site-plan and environmental reviews.

What was approved, and what was not

The planning board approved a special exception

On September 1, 2026, the Bunnell Planning, Zoning and Appeals Board voted 3-1 to approve a special exception for Project Butterfly, according to the Palm Coast Observer. The proposal covers approximately 610 acres at 518 County Road 45, west of Bunnell and north of State Road 100.

The property is currently a sod farm and is zoned Agriculture and Silviculture. Bunnell’s code allows the city to consider a solid-waste management facility in that district through the special-exception process. City staff recommended approval subject to 21 conditions.

The vote was not a construction permit

The planning board’s action did not authorize excavation, landfill construction, truck deliveries or waste disposal. City staff said the applicant would still need major site-plan approval and state environmental permits. Hydrological, geotechnical and traffic studies are expected during later review.

An approved use and an operating landfill are different stages. A renter should not describe the facility as under construction unless a later permit, contractor notice or visible site work confirms it.

Why Flagler County is appealing

The county says important planning questions remain

At its September 14 meeting, the Flagler County Commission unanimously agreed to appeal the special exception, according to FlaglerLive. The Observer’s account of the appeal says county officials raised concerns about compatibility, the comprehensive plan and coordination between governments.

County Commissioner Andy Dance said officials had identified at least four significant issues involving Bunnell’s code and the city and county comprehensive plans. County representatives also objected to the short review window. The applicant, Cocomero LLC, waived economic-development confidentiality at 10:46 a.m. on August 31, a little more than one day before the September 1 hearing.

The next decision belongs to the Bunnell City Commission

The appeal sends the matter to the Bunnell City Commission. If the commission upholds the planning board’s decision, county officials have identified Florida’s Chapter 164 governmental dispute-resolution process as a possible next step before litigation.

That process gives local governments a structured chance to negotiate. The proposal’s status may change through public meetings and written decisions because the September planning vote did not settle the project.

When following the case, distinguish among a proposed use, special-exception approval, site-plan approval, environmental permit and active construction. The labels are not interchangeable.

The renter impacts worth monitoring

Truck routes and traffic volume

The site fronts County Road 45 near State Road 100, so renters should examine those roads first. The applicant’s final traffic study should identify expected daily and peak-hour trips, vehicle types, turning movements, driveway design and any proposed road improvements. The precise hauling corridors remain unverified until that study and an approved route plan become public.

Heavy trucks can affect more than travel time. Check the proposed operating hours, possible school-bus conflicts, turning lanes, pavement responsibilities and whether trucks would pass homes, farms or frequently used intersections. If your routine includes State Road 100 between western Flagler County and Bunnell, compare the eventual route documents with your actual commute instead of relying on a countywide map.

Noise, odor and outdoor use

Noise and odor depend heavily on facility design, distance, wind, operating hours and the type of material accepted. Residents raised both concerns at the September 1 hearing, although their testimony does not establish that a particular home will experience either problem.

In later applications, look for the proposed working face, internal haul roads, equipment locations, buffers, berms, odor-control procedures and truck-arrival hours. Those details will say more about a rental’s potential exposure than the site’s total acreage.

Water, drainage and environmental safeguards

Residents and officials also raised concerns about groundwater, farms and drainage. Those subjects require technical review, and they are not evidence that contamination has occurred. The expected hydrological and geotechnical studies should describe groundwater conditions, stormwater management, soils, liners, leachate collection and monitoring provisions.

If a rental uses a private well, ask the owner when it was last tested and keep the result with your lease records. For a home on public water, identify the utility listed on the bill. A proposed landfill nearby does not prove there is a drinking-water problem. Knowing the water source provides a baseline if a documented issue later arises.

How to check a rental before signing

Map the address against the site and roads

Begin with the proposed site at County Road 45, north of State Road 100. Plot the rental along with your workplace, school or child-care destination, grocery route and any daily medical trips. Repeat the exercise after the release of a formal traffic study or haul-route exhibit.

Straight-line distance tells only part of the story. A house may be farther from the property but closer to a truck route. Another may be nearer the site yet separated from operations by buffers or access patterns. If an approved site plan is eventually issued, it will be more useful than a broad parcel outline.

Visit at practical times

Drive through the area on a weekday morning, during the afternoon commute and after a strong rain. Note the existing truck traffic, road width, standing water, visibility at turns and the sound level inside the house with the windows closed.

Ask nearby residents neutral, specific questions: What types of trucks use this road now? Does water remain in the roadside swale after storms? Is there existing agricultural or industrial noise? Avoid asking whether the landfill has already harmed the area because it is not operating.

Questions current tenants should ask

Ask the landlord for facts tied to the property

A landlord may not know more than the public record, but these questions can uncover information specific to the property:

  • Is the home served by a private well or a public utility?
  • Has the owner received a construction, access, drainage or right-of-way notice concerning the property?
  • Are there recorded easements affecting the driveway or road frontage?
  • Has the owner documented recurring drainage, odor, vibration or access complaints that predate this proposal?
  • Who should the tenant contact if construction blocks normal access or causes property damage?
  • Does the lease require written notice through a portal, email or another method?

Create a dated baseline

Photograph the driveway, roadside ditch or swale, exterior walls and any existing cracks. Record ordinary indoor noise for your own reference, and save water-test documents if the home has a well. This baseline can help distinguish a new condition from one that existed at move-in.

Report future problems in writing and stick to observable facts, including the date, time, duration, location, photographs and effect on access or use of the home. “Six trucks passed between these times” is more useful than “traffic was terrible,” but record only a count you personally observed.

Neighborhood demand without guessing about rents

Some renters may pause while others may not

A disputed landfill proposal can affect how prospective tenants view western Flagler County. One renter may prioritize rural space and accept the uncertainty. Another may avoid a possible hauling corridor because of remote work, outdoor living or a child’s daily schedule.

Those preferences do not establish a future increase or decrease in rent, demand or property value. Such outcomes depend on the appeal, final design, permits, route decisions, construction timing and actual operation. No verified evidence cited here supports assigning a price effect to a specific rental.

Use lease flexibility as a practical tool

If the uncertainty materially affects your decision, compare renewal length, early-termination language and notice requirements. A shorter commitment may give you flexibility, but it can reduce rate certainty or availability. Weigh that tradeoff alongside the commute, condition, water source and total monthly cost instead of making the proposal your only criterion.

For a longer lease, ask whether any unusual access or construction provision shifts responsibilities to the tenant. Do not rely on a verbal promise that future noise, traffic or road work will never occur.

Records and meetings to follow

Use a simple monitoring list

There is no need to follow every rumor. Check these official and reported sources at each project milestone:

  1. Bunnell City Commission: Look for the county’s appeal, the hearing notice, staff report and written decision through the city’s agendas and minutes page.
  2. Bunnell Planning, Zoning and Appeals Board: Watch for a major site-plan hearing, traffic exhibits and technical studies. The city’s calendar confirms that the original PZA hearing occurred at 6 p.m. on September 1, 2026.
  3. Flagler County Commission: Review meeting notices, agendas, summaries and recordings through the county’s meeting portal. The county lists 386-313-4001 and Agendas@FlaglerCounty.gov for meeting information.
  4. Florida Department of Environmental Protection: Review the department’s Central District permitting page for solid-waste permitting information, then verify any application or issued permit.
  5. Local reporting: Compare meeting coverage with the underlying staff reports and decisions. News accounts can alert readers to a change, while official documents control the project’s legal status.

What to record from each update

For each new document, record the date, deciding agency, application type, vote or permit status, appeal deadline, proposed operating hours, access road and next hearing. Save the actual PDF instead of a social-media screenshot with no context.

If an agenda uses technical language, call the listed planning or meeting contact and ask one narrow question: “Does this item authorize construction, or is it another review step?” The answer often clears up the main uncertainty.

FAQ

Has the Project Butterfly landfill been fully approved?

No. Bunnell’s planning board approved a special exception on September 1, 2026, but the decision did not authorize construction or operation. Major site-plan approval, technical review and state environmental permitting would still be required.

What did Flagler County commissioners decide?

On September 14, 2026, county commissioners unanimously agreed to appeal the planning board’s special-exception approval. The appeal goes to the Bunnell City Commission.

Where is the proposed landfill site?

The proposal covers approximately 610 acres at 518 County Road 45, west of Bunnell and north of State Road 100. Renters should use later traffic and site-plan documents to identify the actual access and hauling routes.

What should renters monitor most closely?

Watch for the traffic study, proposed truck routes and operating hours, road improvements, buffers, odor controls, drainage plans, groundwater safeguards and the status of site-plan and environmental permits.

Does the proposal mean nearby rents or property values will fall?

No verified evidence cited in this article establishes a future price effect. Housing demand will depend on the appeal outcome, final design, permits, access routes, construction timing and any actual operating conditions.

How can a tenant document a concern?

Create a dated move-in baseline with photographs of the driveway, drainage areas and existing damage. Report later issues in writing with dates, times, duration, location and photographs. Keep copies of notices and water-test results.
