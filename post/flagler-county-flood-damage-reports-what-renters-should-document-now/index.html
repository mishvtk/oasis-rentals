Flagler County Flood Damage Reports: What Renters Should Document Now

If your Flagler County rental flooded in November 2026, photograph and record the damage before cleanup, stay clear of electrical and contaminated-water hazards, and notify your landlord in writing immediately. Contact your insurance carrier separately, keep every related receipt, and submit one report for the affected address through Flagler County’s Public Damage Assessment Tool.

Each step has its own purpose. Your landlord needs notice to address the building. Your insurer needs evidence for any covered personal-property or lodging claim. Flagler County uses the reports to measure the impact across the community.

Why Flagler County is requesting damage reports

The request follows the November flooding

Palm Coast declared a local state of emergency on November 20, 2026, after heavy rainfall left standing water in streets, swales and low-lying areas. The city said the declaration allowed it to respond more quickly, obtain emergency resources and protect residents and public infrastructure.

On November 25, Flagler County Emergency Management asked residents with flood-damaged homes to submit reports. The reports show officials where damage occurred, how severe it was and which community needs might not be apparent from road inspections.

How to complete the county report

Use the Flagler County Public Damage Assessment Tool, or visit the county’s Emergency Management page and select the tool. The county requests one survey per impacted address. Place the map pin on the affected building or property and include photographs when possible.

If you cannot use the online form, call Flagler County Emergency Management with questions at 386-313-4200. Coordinate with your landlord or property manager to avoid multiple reports for the same rental address.

The county assessment is not an insurance claim, an application for disaster benefits or a request for individual assistance. Complete those processes separately.

Start with safety, not the camera

Standing water and electricity are a dangerous combination

Do not step into standing water to reach a breaker, unplug an appliance or get a better photograph. The CDC’s flooded-home guidance says to shut off power only if you can reach the main switch from a dry location. Otherwise, call an electrician. The electrical system should be checked before power is restored.

Leave immediately if you smell gas or see major structural movement, cracked walls, a sagging ceiling, downed lines or a tree resting on the building. Use a battery-powered light instead of a candle or anything else that could create a spark. Call emergency services if there is an immediate threat, and tell your landlord where you are.

Treat floodwater and wet materials as contaminated

Floodwater may contain sewage, chemicals, sharp debris or organisms that can cause illness. Keep children and pets away. If officials and qualified professionals say it is safe to enter, wear waterproof boots, gloves, eye protection and appropriate respiratory protection during mold cleanup.

Do not turn on a wet HVAC system, fan, vacuum, refrigerator or other appliance. The CDC advises having a flooded HVAC system inspected and cleaned before use because contaminated equipment can circulate mold through the home.

Do not create another road hazard

Do not drive around barricades or enter a road because another vehicle made it through. During the flooding, Palm Coast warned that water could conceal dangerous road conditions. Check city or county road-closure information and wait for a safe route.

Build a room-by-room evidence record

Once you can enter safely, record one continuous video from the exterior through every affected room. Follow it with clear still photographs. Start with wide views that identify the room, take medium views of each damaged area, then get close-ups of water lines, stains, swelling, cracks and labels.

What to capture in every room

  • Water level: Photograph visible water lines beside a ruler or another fixed reference. Include doors, baseboards, cabinets and furniture legs.
  • Floors: Record lifting planks, buckled laminate, loose tile, saturated carpet and water beneath flooring transitions.
  • Walls: Capture stains, bubbling paint, soft drywall, detached baseboards and water entering around windows or doors.
  • Ceilings: Photograph stains, cracks, bulges and dripping areas from a safe distance. Do not stand beneath a sagging ceiling.
  • Closets and cabinets: Open them and photograph the interior. FEMA specifically recommends documenting these concealed spaces.
  • Utilities: Record the visible condition of outlets, the electrical panel, water heater, HVAC equipment and any sewage backup without touching wet components.

Belongings, appliances and vehicles

  • Furniture and electronics: Photograph each item where you found it, then photograph the brand, model and serial-number label if you can reach it safely.
  • Clothing, books and household goods: Group similar items for an overview, then photograph higher-value or unusual pieces separately.
  • Appliances: Capture the entire appliance, the depth of the water exposure and the manufacturer label. Do not test it.
  • Vehicles: Photograph the exterior water line, cabin, trunk and visible damage. Record where the vehicle was parked and contact the auto insurer separately.
  • Receipts or proof of ownership: Preserve order confirmations, bank records, photographs from before the flood and product manuals.

Exterior and shared areas

From safe ground, photograph the front, rear and sides of the home, along with the direction the water appears to have come from. Record standing water, blocked drainage, damaged fencing, displaced outdoor equipment, roof or soffit damage and debris resting against the structure.

At a duplex, townhome or multifamily property, photograph hallways, garages and other shared areas only if you can do so without entering restricted or unsafe spaces. You can note damage to neighboring properties for context, but do not photograph people through windows or enter another resident’s home.

Create a written timeline while details are fresh

Use one running incident log

Use a note, spreadsheet or paper log to record events in order. Include when the rain or water intrusion became noticeable, when you left or returned, when the power went out, and when you contacted the landlord, insurer or county. Use exact dates and times when you know them. Clearly mark estimates instead of guessing.

For every call, note the number dialed, the person or department reached, the advice you received and any reference number. Save emails, portal messages, text conversations and voicemail. Back up the log and photographs somewhere outside the damaged device or home.

Inventory damaged property carefully

Use the same columns throughout your inventory: room, item, brand, model, approximate purchase date, original cost if known, visible damage, photograph filename and disposal status. Do not inflate values or claim certainty you do not have. An old online order or bank transaction may help establish when you bought an item and that you owned it.

FEMA’s damage-documentation guidance recommends photographing structural and personal-property damage inside and outside, recording appliance serial numbers, keeping replacement and repair receipts, and retaining representative material samples when an insurer requests them.

Track every flood-related expense

Keep a separate folder for hotel or temporary lodging invoices, meals required by displacement, laundry, transportation, cleaning supplies, storage, replacement necessities and professional inspections. Photograph paper receipts before the ink fades. Ask your carrier which expenses might qualify under your policy before counting on reimbursement.

Keep written repair requests and appointment records as well. If maintenance personnel enter the rental, note the date, what work they performed and any equipment they installed or removed. When it is safe, photograph the condition before and after each visit.

Send three separate reports

Landlord or property manager: the building and repairs

Send written notice using the method listed in your lease, such as the maintenance portal or designated email. Include the rental address, affected rooms, active hazards, whether the home is occupied and your contact information. Attach a manageable group of clear photographs and offer access to the full evidence folder.

Ask the landlord to confirm receipt and provide safe-occupancy instructions, a repair plan, and any entry or contractor schedule. Avoid authorizing structural, electrical, plumbing or mold-remediation work yourself unless the lease, landlord and insurer clearly permit it.

If casualty damage has substantially impaired your use of the rental, Florida Statute 83.63 addresses termination or vacating an unusable portion under specified circumstances. The outcome depends on the specific facts. Read the current statute, review your lease and get qualified legal advice before withholding rent, moving out permanently or treating the lease as ended.

Insurance carrier: belongings and covered expenses

Contact your renters-insurance carrier promptly and ask whether the reported cause of loss is covered. Request the claim number, adjuster’s contact information, documentation checklist, inspection requirements and disposal instructions. Coverage depends on the policy, endorsements and cause, so use the carrier’s written response instead of relying on a neighbor’s experience.

Upload the inventory, photographs, video and receipts in the carrier’s requested format. Keep an untouched copy of every original file and a copy of everything you submit. If more damage appears while walls and flooring dry, take dated photographs and notify the adjuster.

Flagler County: the community impact

Complete the county assessment even if you have contacted the landlord and insurer. Choose the damage description that best matches what you can see, place the address pin correctly and add useful photographs. Do not classify concealed structural damage you are not qualified to evaluate.

Emergency Management uses the county reports to understand the extent and location of the damage. A report does not replace a maintenance request, activate insurance coverage or guarantee assistance.

What not to discard too soon

Photograph first, then confirm the rules

Do not immediately discard flooring, rugs, furniture, electronics, appliance labels, damaged documents or other claim evidence. Photograph each item where you found it, record its condition, and ask the insurer whether an adjuster needs to inspect it. FEMA advises preserving samples of materials such as carpet, flooring, wallpaper or upholstery when practical and requested.

When it is safe, separate damaged items from undamaged property. Label anything you move by room so its original location remains clear. Do not leave wet property where it blocks an exit, creates a fall hazard or spreads contamination.

Health hazards are the exception

Perishable food and contaminated absorbent materials may need to be discarded promptly. Before removing them, photograph the items and their location if you can do so safely. Then follow instructions from public-health officials, the landlord and the insurer. Never keep a hazardous object indoors solely as claim evidence.

If you must remove an item before the adjuster responds, photograph it from several angles, record why you disposed of it and keep a small material sample only if it is safe. Save municipal debris instructions and disposal receipts.

A practical sequence for Palm Coast renters

  1. Move people and pets away from standing water, unstable materials and electrical hazards.
  2. Notify the landlord or property manager in writing and report any urgent safety condition.
  3. Call the insurance carrier and obtain claim-specific documentation and disposal instructions.
  4. Once entry is safe, make the exterior-to-interior video and room-by-room photographs.
  5. Create the property inventory, incident timeline and expense folder.
  6. Coordinate with the owner or manager, then submit one county assessment for the affected address.
  7. Continue photographing changes, repairs and newly visible damage.

For nonemergency flooding or drainage concerns within Palm Coast, the city directs residents to Palm Coast Connect. The city’s November emergency notice also listed Customer Service at 386-986-2360, including for sewage backups involving Palm Coast PEP systems.

FAQ

Should a Flagler County renter submit the county damage survey or should the landlord do it?

Coordinate with the landlord or property manager because Flagler County requests only one survey per impacted address. A renter can report the damage, but everyone involved should avoid duplicate submissions.

Does the Public Damage Assessment Tool start an insurance claim?

No. Flagler County says the tool measures community impacts. It is not an insurance claim, request for assistance or application for disaster benefits. Contact your insurer separately.

What should I photograph before flood cleanup?

Take wide and close views of every affected room, visible water lines, floors, walls, ceilings, cabinets, closets, furniture, appliances, serial-number labels, vehicles and exterior areas. Photograph hazardous items only from a safe location.

Can I throw away wet belongings before an insurance adjuster arrives?

When practical, ask the insurer for disposal instructions first. Photograph each item and its original location before removing it. Health hazards may require prompt disposal, so do not keep contaminated property indoors solely as evidence.

What should my written notice to the landlord include?

Include the rental address, when you discovered the problem, affected rooms, active hazards, whether the home is occupied, photographs and your contact information. Ask for written confirmation, safe-occupancy instructions and the repair schedule.

Can I stop paying rent if part of my rental is unusable?

Do not make that decision based on general guidance. Florida Statute 83.63 addresses casualty damage, but the outcome depends on the facts and lease. Review the statute and get qualified legal advice before withholding rent or ending the tenancy.
