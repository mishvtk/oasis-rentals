Security Deposit Rules in Florida: What Broward, Palm Beach, and Flagler County Renters Need to Know Before Move-Out

Moving out of a rental home or apartment can be stressful, especially when a security deposit is involved. For renters in Broward County, Palm Beach County, and Flagler County, understanding Florida's security-deposit rules can help prevent surprises and make it easier to protect your money.

Florida Statute §83.49 establishes important requirements for how residential security deposits are held, how landlords must notify tenants about deductions, and when deposits must be returned.

Although local rental practices can vary, these basic security-deposit requirements apply throughout Florida, including Broward, Palm Beach, and Flagler counties.



What Is a Security Deposit?

A security deposit is money a tenant provides to a landlord at the beginning of a rental agreement. Its purpose is to help protect the landlord against certain financial losses that may occur during the tenancy.

In Florida, security deposits are generally governed by state law, including Florida Statutes Section 83.49, which establishes requirements for landlords regarding the handling and return of security deposits.

A security deposit is not automatically money the landlord can keep when a tenant moves out. Generally, a landlord may have grounds to make a claim against the deposit when the tenant has certain unpaid financial obligations or causes damage beyond normal wear and tear.

What Can a Security Deposit Potentially Cover?

Depending on the lease and circumstances, a landlord may potentially make a claim against a security deposit for:

  • Damage beyond ordinary wear and tear

    Tenants are generally responsible for damage that goes beyond the normal deterioration expected from ordinary use of a rental property.

  • Unpaid rent

    If rent remains unpaid when the tenancy ends, the landlord may be able to apply some or all of the deposit toward the amount owed, subject to Florida law and the lease.

  • Certain other amounts owed under the rental agreement

    A lease may establish other financial responsibilities for the tenant, provided those provisions are consistent with applicable law.

  • Other legitimate claims permitted by the lease and Florida law

    The specific circumstances matter, so renters should review both their lease and Florida's landlord-tenant requirements.

Ordinary Wear and Tear vs. Damage

One of the most important distinctions for renters is the difference between ordinary wear and tear and actual damage.

Normal wear can occur simply because a property is lived in over time. Examples might include minor signs of use that naturally develop during an ordinary tenancy. Damage, on the other hand, may involve conditions caused by misuse, neglect, or actions beyond normal residential use.

Because disputes can arise over the property's condition, renters should document the home carefully when they move in and again before moving out. Photos and videos can provide a useful record of the property's condition.

Review Your Lease Before Moving Out

Before handing over the keys, renters should carefully review their lease and look for any move-out requirements.

The lease may contain requirements concerning:

  • Cleaning

  • Returning keys, remotes, or access devices

  • Paying outstanding utilities

  • Landscaping or yard maintenance

  • Removing personal belongings

  • Pet-related responsibilities

  • Repairing tenant-caused damage

  • Providing a forwarding address

  • Giving the required notice before moving out

Following these requirements can help reduce misunderstandings and make the move-out process smoother.

Florida Security Deposit Rules Matter

Florida law establishes specific procedures for landlords who receive security deposits, including requirements related to how deposits are held and how landlords must provide notice concerning claims against the deposit. The rules can depend on whether the landlord is making a claim against the deposit and the circumstances surrounding the tenancy.

For that reason, renters should not assume that a deposit will automatically be returned in full, but they also should not assume that a landlord can keep the deposit without following Florida's requirements.

Tips for Protecting Your Security Deposit

Before moving out, consider taking these steps:

  1. Read your lease again. Look specifically for move-out instructions.

  2. Clean the property according to the lease requirements.

  3. Repair tenant-caused damage when appropriate.

  4. Take dated photos and videos of each room after the property has been cleaned.

  5. Return all keys and access devices.

  6. Pay outstanding rent or other legitimate charges.

  7. Keep copies of receipts and communications with the landlord.

  8. Provide your forwarding address when required or appropriate so the landlord can send correspondence concerning the deposit.

A security deposit is designed to provide financial protection for landlords while giving tenants a clear financial stake in meeting their responsibilities under the rental agreement. Understanding what the deposit may cover, carefully following the lease, and documenting the property's condition can help Florida renters avoid unnecessary disputes when moving out.

For renters in Broward, Palm Beach, Flagler County, and other parts of Florida, reviewing the specific lease terms alongside Florida's landlord-tenant laws is an important part of preparing for move-out.



Florida’s 15-Day Security Deposit Rule: What Renters Need to Know

One of the most important deadlines Florida renters should understand before moving out is the 15-day rule for security deposits. Florida law establishes specific requirements for landlords regarding the return of a tenant’s security deposit after a rental agreement ends.

What Is Florida’s 15-Day Rule?

Under Florida Statutes Section 83.49, when a landlord does not intend to impose a claim against the security deposit, the landlord generally must return the deposit, together with any required interest, within 15 days after the termination of the rental agreement.

For renters, this makes it important to keep clear records of the date the tenancy ended and when possession of the property was surrendered.

When Does the 15-Day Period Begin?

The deadline is tied to the termination of the rental agreement. Tenants should keep documentation that can establish when they officially moved out and surrendered possession.

Useful records may include:

  • A copy of the lease showing the ending date

  • Written notice to the landlord

  • Emails or text messages confirming the move-out date

  • Photos or videos taken when the property was vacated

  • Documentation showing that keys, access cards, or remotes were returned

  • A move-out inspection report, if one was completed

Keeping these records can make it easier to establish the relevant dates if there is later a disagreement about the deposit.

What If the Landlord Wants to Keep Part of the Deposit?

The 15-day rule applies when the landlord does not intend to make a claim against the security deposit. If the landlord intends to claim part or all of the deposit, Florida law provides a different notification process.

Generally, the landlord must send the tenant written notice of the intention to impose a claim within 30 days after the rental agreement ends. The statute also establishes requirements concerning the tenant's opportunity to object to the claim.

Because the rules differ depending on whether the landlord is returning the deposit or making a claim, renters should not assume that every security-deposit dispute can be resolved simply by counting 15 days.

What Happens If the Deposit Is Not Returned Within 15 Days?

If the landlord does not return the deposit within the applicable period, that does not automatically mean the tenant has forfeited the deposit.

Florida's security-deposit statute contains additional requirements governing notices, claims, and the handling of deposits. The specific circumstances can determine what rights and obligations apply.

Renters who believe their deposit has been improperly withheld should keep all relevant documents and review the applicable provisions of Florida law.

Keep a Move-Out Paper Trail

One of the best ways to protect yourself is to create a detailed record before leaving the rental property.

Before moving out, consider:

  1. Review your lease for move-out requirements.

  2. Provide the required notice to the landlord.

  3. Document the property's condition with dated photos and videos.

  4. Clean the property according to the lease terms.

  5. Return all keys and access devices.

  6. Keep proof of the date possession was surrendered.

  7. Save copies of communications with the landlord.

  8. Keep your forwarding address updated with the landlord.

These steps can be particularly helpful if a dispute later arises over the timing or amount of a security-deposit return.

The Bottom Line for Florida Renters

Florida's 15-day rule is an important protection for tenants whose landlord does not intend to make a claim against the security deposit. When applicable, the landlord generally must return the deposit and required interest within 15 days after termination of the rental agreement.

However, security-deposit disputes can involve additional statutory deadlines and requirements. Renters should therefore document their move-out date, preserve evidence of the property's condition, and keep records of all communications with the landlord.


What Happens If the Landlord Wants to Keep Part of the Security Deposit?

Moving out of a rental property does not always mean a tenant will receive the entire security deposit back. In Florida, a landlord may seek to deduct certain amounts from a security deposit when the landlord has a legitimate claim under the rental agreement and applicable law.

However, Florida law establishes specific deadlines and notice requirements that landlords must follow when making a claim against a security deposit.

The Landlord Has 30 Days to Provide Notice

Under Florida Statutes § 83.49, if a landlord intends to impose a claim against a security deposit, the landlord generally must provide the tenant with written notice within 30 days after termination of the rental agreement.

The notice must state the landlord's intention to make a claim and explain the reason for the claim. Under the current statute, the notice may be provided by certified mail to the tenant's last known mailing address or by email in accordance with the applicable statutory requirements.

This makes it important for tenants to provide the landlord with a current mailing address or other required contact information when they move out.

The Notice Must Explain the Proposed Deduction

A proper claim notice is not simply a message saying that the landlord is keeping part of the deposit. Florida law requires the notice to identify the landlord's intention to impose a claim and the reason for it.

For example, a landlord might claim an amount related to damage beyond ordinary wear and tear, unpaid rent, or another amount that the landlord believes is owed under the rental agreement.

Tenants should carefully review the explanation and compare it with their lease, move-in documentation, photographs, receipts, and other records.

Tenants Have 15 Days to Object

One of the most important parts of Florida's security-deposit procedure is the 15-day objection period.

If the tenant disagrees with the landlord's proposed deduction or the amount claimed, the tenant generally has 15 days after receiving the notice to object in writing. The landlord's notice is required to inform the tenant of this deadline and provide the address where the objection should be sent.

Because the deadline is short, tenants should not ignore a security-deposit claim.

If you believe a deduction is incorrect, respond in writing and keep a copy of your response along with proof of when it was sent.

What If the Tenant Does Not Object?

If the tenant does not object to the claim or the amount within the 15-day period, the landlord may proceed with the deduction. Florida law also provides that the landlord must remit any remaining balance of the deposit to the tenant within 30 days after the date of the notice of intention to impose the claim.

Importantly, failing to object within the 15-day period does not necessarily eliminate every possible right the tenant may have. The statute states that the failure to make a timely objection does not waive the tenant's right to seek damages in a separate action.

What If the Landlord Misses the 30-Day Deadline?

Florida law also addresses situations where the landlord fails to provide the required claim notice within the 30-day period.

Generally, the landlord forfeits the right to impose a claim against the security deposit itself. The statute provides that the landlord may not seek a setoff against the deposit, although the landlord may still be able to pursue a separate action for damages after returning the deposit.

This is one reason tenants should keep records showing when the rental agreement ended and when they surrendered possession of the property.

Keep Your Move-Out Records

If you receive a claim against your security deposit, having good documentation can make it easier to understand and dispute the deduction.

Consider keeping:

  • A copy of the signed lease

  • Your move-in inspection or condition report

  • Photos or videos of the property

  • Photos taken shortly before moving out

  • Receipts for cleaning or repairs

  • Proof that rent and other charges were paid

  • Written communications with the landlord or property manager

  • A copy of the landlord's security-deposit notice

  • A copy of any written objection you send

Good records can help establish the property's condition and clarify what happened before and after the tenancy ended.

Don't Ignore a Security-Deposit Claim

Receiving a notice that your landlord wants to keep part of your security deposit can be frustrating, particularly if you believe the deduction is unreasonable.

The most important thing is to pay attention to the deadlines. Review the landlord's explanation, compare the claim with your lease and move-out records, and respond in writing if you disagree.

For Florida renters, the 30-day landlord notice period and 15-day tenant objection period are especially important. Understanding these deadlines before moving out can help you protect your rights and avoid missing an opportunity to challenge a disputed deduction.


What If the Landlord Misses the 30-Day Notice Deadline?

Florida law provides an important protection for tenants when a landlord wants to make a claim against a security deposit.

Under Florida Statutes § 83.49, a landlord generally has 30 days after the termination of the rental agreement to notify the tenant in writing of an intention to impose a claim against the security deposit. The notice must explain the reason for the claim and follow the statutory requirements.

What Happens If the 30-Day Deadline Is Missed?

If the landlord fails to provide the required notice within the statutory 30-day period, Florida law generally provides that the landlord forfeits the right to impose a claim against the security deposit.

This can be an important protection for renters. A tenant who is expecting a security deposit refund should keep records showing when the rental agreement ended and when possession of the property was returned.

For example, if a tenant moves out and properly surrenders the property, documenting the move-out date can help establish when the landlord's 30-day notice period began.

Missing the Deadline Does Not Necessarily End Every Dispute

Although missing the notice deadline can prevent a landlord from making a claim against the security deposit, renters should not assume that every potential dispute automatically disappears.

Florida law provides that the landlord may still be able to pursue a separate action for damages after returning the deposit. In other words, losing the ability to claim against the security deposit does not necessarily mean that a landlord can never seek damages through another legal process.

This distinction is important because a security-deposit dispute and a separate claim for damages can involve different legal procedures.

Why Tenants Should Keep Good Records

If there is a disagreement about a security deposit, documentation can be extremely useful.

Tenants should consider keeping:

  • A copy of the rental agreement

  • Proof of the move-out date

  • Move-in and move-out inspection records

  • Photos or videos showing the property's condition

  • Copies of rent-payment records

  • Receipts for cleaning or repairs

  • Emails, texts, or letters exchanged with the landlord

  • A copy of any security-deposit notice received

  • Proof of the date the notice was received

These records can help establish important dates and provide evidence about the condition of the property.

Check the Dates Carefully

When dealing with a security-deposit claim, the dates matter.

A tenant should determine:

  1. When the rental agreement terminated

  2. When possession of the property was returned

  3. When the landlord sent the notice

  4. When the tenant received the notice

  5. What the notice says about the claimed deduction

If the landlord's notice appears to have been sent after the applicable 30-day period, the tenant may want to carefully review the situation rather than simply accepting the deduction.

Consider Legal Advice for Significant Disputes

Security-deposit disputes can involve more than just the amount of money being withheld. The lease, condition of the property, communications between the parties, and timing of the landlord's actions can all matter.

If a significant amount of money is involved or the landlord is pursuing additional damages, tenants may want to obtain advice from a qualified Florida attorney or an appropriate legal-aid organization.

Understanding the 30-day notice requirement is an important part of protecting your rights as a Florida renter, but individual circumstances can affect how the law applies.


Renters Have 15 Days to Object to a Security-Deposit Claim

Receiving a notice that your landlord intends to keep part of your security deposit can be stressful, especially if you believe the proposed deduction is incorrect or unreasonable.

Florida law provides tenants with an important opportunity to respond. If a landlord provides a valid notice stating an intention to impose a claim against the security deposit, the tenant generally has 15 days after receiving the notice to object in writing.

Because this deadline is relatively short, renters should review the notice promptly and respond if they disagree with the proposed deduction.

What Should a Tenant Do After Receiving the Notice?

Start by carefully reviewing the landlord's notice. Look at the reason given for the proposed deduction and compare it with your lease, move-in records, and the condition of the property when you moved out.

If you disagree with the claim, your objection should be made in writing and should clearly explain what you dispute.

For example, you might dispute:

  • The amount being deducted

  • The condition of the property described by the landlord

  • Charges for damage that existed before you moved in

  • Cleaning charges that you believe are not justified

  • Repair costs that appear unrelated to your tenancy

  • Charges that you believe are inconsistent with the lease

Keep the objection factual and focused on the specific issues you disagree with.

Keep a Copy of Your Objection

Do not send a written objection and then discard your records.

Keep a copy of the objection and documentation showing when and how it was sent. If possible, use a communication method that provides evidence of delivery or receipt.

Your records can become particularly important if the disagreement continues or develops into a formal legal dispute.

Documentation Can Support Your Position

Good documentation can help establish the condition of the property and the financial obligations under the lease.

Useful records may include:

  • Move-in photographs: Pictures taken before or shortly after moving in can document pre-existing conditions.

  • Move-out photographs: These can show the condition of the property when you left.

  • Videos: A move-out video can provide additional visual documentation.

  • Inspection reports: Keep copies of move-in and move-out inspection documents.

  • Cleaning receipts: These can show that cleaning was completed before the property was surrendered.

  • Repair receipts: Documentation of repairs can help demonstrate work that you paid for yourself.

  • The lease: Review the lease to understand your responsibilities and any provisions concerning the security deposit.

  • Rent-payment records: Keep evidence showing that rent and other required payments were made.

  • Emails and text messages: Communications with the landlord or property manager may help establish what was discussed.

  • The landlord's security-deposit notice: Keep the original notice and any related correspondence.

Why Move-In and Move-Out Photos Matter

Photographs can be especially helpful in security-deposit disputes.

For example, if a landlord claims that a wall, appliance, floor, or fixture was damaged during the tenancy, move-in photographs may help establish whether the condition was already present.

Similarly, move-out photographs can document the property's condition when the tenant returned possession.

When possible, renters should take clear photographs of each room and important features of the property before moving out.

Put Important Communications in Writing

Even if you initially discuss the issue by phone, consider following up with a written message that summarizes what was discussed.

Written communication can create a clearer record of the dispute and reduce misunderstandings about what each party said or agreed to.

Avoid relying solely on verbal conversations when dealing with an important security-deposit disagreement.

Don't Ignore the 15-Day Deadline

The 15-day objection period is an important part of Florida's security-deposit procedure.

If you believe the landlord's proposed deduction is incorrect, review the notice immediately and make sure your written objection is submitted within the applicable deadline.

Keep copies of everything you send and receive.


Give Your Landlord Your New Address Before Moving Out

Moving to a new home involves more than packing boxes and returning the keys. If you are expecting your security deposit to be returned, one important step is providing your landlord with your new mailing address before or when you move out.

Florida's security-deposit law specifically addresses the tenant's responsibility to provide a new address so the landlord can send notices and other communications concerning the deposit.

Why Your Forwarding Address Matters

Your landlord may need to send you important information about your security deposit after you leave the rental property.

For example, if the landlord intends to impose a claim against your security deposit, Florida law establishes specific notice requirements. Having your correct forwarding address helps ensure that you can receive important correspondence after the tenancy ends.

Providing your new address also creates a record showing that you took steps to make yourself reachable after moving out.

Give the Address in Writing

Whenever possible, provide your forwarding address in writing.

You might include it in your move-out notice, provide it separately to the landlord or property manager, or use another method permitted under your lease and the landlord's procedures.

Keep a copy of whatever you send.

Your records could include:

  • A copy of your move-out notice

  • The forwarding address you provided

  • The date you provided it

  • Email confirmation or other proof of delivery

  • Written communications with the landlord or property manager

Keeping proof can be helpful if there is later a disagreement about whether the landlord had your correct address.

Moving to Another Florida County?

Your new address does not have to be in the same county as your old rental.

For example, if you are moving from Broward County to Palm Beach County, from Palm Beach County to Flagler County, or from Florida to another state, make sure your landlord has your complete and accurate forwarding address.

Include all necessary information, such as:

  • Street address

  • Apartment or unit number

  • City

  • State

  • ZIP code

If you are moving out of Florida entirely, provide the complete address where you can reliably receive mail.

Check Your Lease for Notice Procedures

Your lease may contain specific instructions about how notices must be provided.

Before moving out, review the lease to see whether it identifies:

  • A specific mailing address for notices

  • An email address for communications

  • An online tenant portal

  • Required move-out procedures

  • Electronic-notice provisions

Florida law also contains provisions concerning electronic delivery of certain security-deposit notices when the statutory requirements are met. Therefore, renters should not assume that an email or text message automatically satisfies every legal notice requirement.

Don't Rely Only on the Postal Service

You can also consider arranging mail forwarding with the U.S. Postal Service when you move.

However, changing your mailing address with the postal service should not replace directly providing your landlord with your new address.

Giving the landlord the address directly creates a clearer record and helps ensure that security-deposit correspondence is sent where you expect to receive it.

Keep Your Move-Out Records Together

Your forwarding address should be part of your overall move-out documentation.

Consider keeping one folder containing:

  • Your lease

  • Move-out notice

  • Forwarding address

  • Move-in inspection records

  • Move-out photographs and videos

  • Cleaning and repair receipts

  • Final utility records

  • Rent-payment records

  • Communications with the landlord

  • Security-deposit correspondence

Having these documents together can make it much easier to resolve questions about your deposit after you leave.

A Simple Step That Can Prevent Problems

Providing your forwarding address may seem like a small part of moving, but it can be important when dealing with a security deposit.

Before turning in your keys, make sure your landlord or property manager has your current mailing information, keep proof that you provided it, and review your lease for any specific notice procedures.

A few minutes spent updating your address can help ensure that important security-deposit communications do not get lost after you move.


Broward County Renters: A Move-Out Guide for Protecting Your Security Deposit

Renting a home or apartment in Broward County can come with different building and community procedures, but renters should remember that Florida's statewide landlord-tenant laws establish the basic rules governing residential security deposits.

Whether you live in Fort Lauderdale, Hollywood, Pembroke Pines, Coral Springs, or another Broward County community, understanding your responsibilities before moving out can help reduce misunderstandings and make the security-deposit process easier.

Local property managers, condominium associations, and apartment communities may have their own move-out procedures. However, those procedures should not be confused with Florida's statutory requirements concerning security deposits.

1. Review Your Lease

Before packing your belongings, carefully review your rental agreement.

Pay particular attention to provisions concerning:

  • Notice of your intention to move

  • Lease termination dates

  • Cleaning requirements

  • Maintenance and repair responsibilities

  • Move-out inspections

  • Key and access-device returns

  • Security deposits

  • Forwarding addresses

  • Required communication methods

Following the lease's move-out requirements can help prevent avoidable problems.

2. Confirm the Lease Termination Date

Make sure you know the exact date your rental agreement ends.

The termination date can be important because Florida's security-deposit law establishes certain deadlines based on the termination of the rental agreement.

If you have renewed, extended, or modified your lease, keep copies of those agreements so you can confirm the correct termination date.

3. Complete Required Move-Out Procedures

Different apartment and condominium communities may have their own procedures.

Management might require you to:

  • Schedule a move-out inspection

  • Reserve an elevator

  • Reserve a loading area

  • Schedule a moving truck

  • Obtain parking authorization

  • Return building access cards

  • Return garage remotes

  • Follow specific moving hours

Complete these requirements before your move whenever possible.

These building procedures are separate from Florida's statutory security-deposit rules, but failing to follow them could create additional issues under your lease or community rules.

4. Photograph and Document the Property

Document the property's condition before you leave.

Take clear photographs and videos of the major areas of the rental, including:

  • Living rooms and bedrooms

  • Kitchen and appliances

  • Bathrooms

  • Floors

  • Walls and ceilings

  • Closets

  • Doors and windows

  • Balconies or patios

  • Fixtures

  • Areas that had existing damage

If you have photographs from the beginning of your tenancy, compare them with your move-out documentation.

Keep the original files and organize them with your other rental records.

5. Return Keys, Remotes, and Access Cards

Make sure you return everything that belongs to the landlord or property.

This may include:

  • Apartment keys

  • Mailbox keys

  • Garage remotes

  • Parking permits

  • Building access cards

  • Pool or amenity access devices

Whenever possible, obtain written confirmation that these items were returned.

This is particularly important in apartment and condominium buildings where replacement fees may apply to missing access devices.

6. Provide Your Forwarding Address

Before moving out, give the landlord or property manager your new mailing address.

Florida's security-deposit statute addresses the tenant's obligation to provide a forwarding address so the landlord can send notices concerning the deposit.

Your new address can be outside Broward County, outside Florida, or even outside the United States. What matters is that the landlord has accurate information for contacting you.

Keep a copy of the forwarding address you provided and proof of delivery when possible.

7. Keep Copies of All Communications

Good recordkeeping can make a major difference if questions arise after you move out.

Keep copies of:

  • Your lease

  • Move-out notice

  • Emails with the landlord

  • Text messages

  • Inspection reports

  • Photographs and videos

  • Cleaning receipts

  • Repair receipts

  • Rent-payment records

  • Key-return documentation

  • Forwarding-address information

  • Security-deposit notices

Try to keep important communications in writing rather than relying only on telephone conversations.

8. Monitor the Statutory Deadlines

Moving out does not necessarily end your responsibilities concerning the security deposit.

Florida law establishes specific procedures when a landlord intends to make a claim against a security deposit. Under Florida Statutes § 83.49, a landlord generally has 30 days after termination of the rental agreement to provide written notice of an intention to impose a claim.

If the landlord sends a valid claim notice, the tenant generally has 15 days after receiving the notice to object in writing.

Because these deadlines can be important, renters should continue monitoring their mail and other legally permitted communication methods after moving out.

Don't Confuse Building Procedures With State Law

A Broward County apartment or condominium community may require a tenant to make an elevator reservation, schedule a move-out appointment, return access cards, or follow specific parking procedures.

These requirements may be part of the lease, building rules, or property-management procedures.

However, they are separate from Florida's statewide statutory requirements concerning security deposits.

Understanding the difference can help renters determine which requirements come from their lease or building and which come from Florida law.

Before leaving your rental, consider completing the following checklist:
