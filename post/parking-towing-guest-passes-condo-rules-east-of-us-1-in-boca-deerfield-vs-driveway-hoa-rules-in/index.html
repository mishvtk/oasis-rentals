A. Quick Synopsis — What Differs, in One Line:

Condominiums east of US-1 in Boca Raton and Deerfield Beach typically treat parking as a common-element management function (decals, assigned stalls, guest passes, posted tow notices, and board enforcement), while Palm Coast single-family and HOA communities rely more on recorded covenants and municipal driveway/parking rules—and recent state and local developments have reshaped what HOAs can enforce.

Parking as a Managed Common Element: Boca Raton & Deerfield Beach Condos

In coastal condominium complexes east of US-1, parking is often a shared and tightly regulated element of community life. Because land is limited, most associations assign parking stalls by deed or board policy, issue decals to residents, and require guest passes to prevent unauthorized use. Enforcement is typically handled by the board or property manager under the condominium’s governing documents.

Towing procedures must comply with Florida Statutes §715.07, requiring clear signage, a contract with a licensed towing company, and consistent enforcement. Guest parking is often capped in duration or subject to approval during peak seasons. Many associations now use digital guest systems or license-plate recognition to modernize oversight and reduce conflicts.

HOAs & Single-Family Rules: Palm Coast and Flagler County

In contrast, single-family HOAs in Palm Coast operate under recorded covenants, conditions, and restrictions (CC&Rs) that emphasize driveway standards, street-parking limits, and aesthetic control rather than shared-lot management. Residents generally park on private property, and disputes arise when vehicles are stored long-term, used for commercial purposes, or violate city codes.

Recent updates to Florida’s HOA statute (Chapter 720, F.S.) and municipal ordinances have clarified that associations must follow due process—notice, hearing, and opportunity to cure—before fining or towing. Palm Coast’s municipal code also prohibits parking that obstructs sidewalks or rights-of-way, reinforcing public enforcement alongside HOA rules.

Evolving Legal Landscape

Both regions are adapting to statewide shifts in how associations regulate vehicle use. Legislative changes in 2024 expanded owners’ rights to challenge board enforcement actions and required clearer notice before towing from common areas. Boards are also encouraged to standardize guest-pass systems and coordinate with local law enforcement to avoid due-process violations.

For property managers and residents alike, understanding whether parking is a common-area privilege (as in east-coast condos) or a private-property covenant (as in Palm Coast HOAs) is crucial for compliance and harmony in Florida’s diverse community-association landscape.



B. Who controls parking (condo common elements vs HOA private lots & driveways)

  • Condo Parking Rules East of US-1: How Boca Raton and Deerfield Beach Associations Manage Common Elements

In condominium communities east of US-1 in Boca Raton and Deerfield Beach, parking is one of the most regulated aspects of daily living — and for good reason. Limited land, high unit density, and shared ownership structures make parking areas essential common elements under the condominium declaration. That means they are not individually owned, but rather jointly maintained and governed by the association on behalf of all residents.

Parking as a Common Element

Under Chapter 718, Florida Statutes (the Condominium Act), parking spaces are classified either as common elements or limited common elements depending on the declaration. In most east-of-US-1 properties — where garages are rare and space is at a premium — parking lots are almost always common elements. This gives the condominium board authority to regulate use through rules and regulations, ensuring fair access and order among residents.

Board Authority: Permits, Decals & Assigned Spaces

The condominium board, acting through its adopted rules, typically manages all aspects of parking operations. This includes issuing parking permits or decals, assigning numbered stalls, and setting guest-parking procedures. Guest parking is often limited by time, frequency, or advance registration requirements, especially in oceanfront or high-demand buildings. Some communities have moved to electronic monitoring or plate-recognition systems to improve enforcement accuracy.

Enforcement: Fines, Towing & Rule Compliance

Enforcement of parking rules flows from both the association’s governing documents and Chapter 718. Boards may impose fines for violations after providing notice and an opportunity for a hearing. Towing or decal removal is also permitted if consistent with state law — specifically, Florida Statute §715.07, which governs vehicle removal from private property. To comply, condominiums must post proper signage, contract only with licensed towing companies, and apply rules uniformly.

In most Boca and Deerfield condos, boards coordinate closely with community association managers to maintain compliance, handle disputes, and communicate policy updates to residents. This structured framework ensures the balance between resident convenience and property order — a necessity in densely populated, high-value coastal condominiums.

In Palm Coast’s single-family and HOA communities, driveway and street parking rules reflect a balance between neighborhood aesthetics, homeowner rights, and city code enforcement. Unlike condominiums where parking areas are shared common elements, Palm Coast homeowners typically own their driveways outright. As a result, HOAs can regulate parking only to the extent allowed by their recorded covenants and consistent with municipal ordinances.

Homeowners’ associations in Palm Coast derive their parking authority from recorded covenants, conditions, and restrictions (CC&Rs). These documents spell out what types of vehicles can be parked on private property, when guest vehicles are allowed, and whether boats, RVs, or trailers can be stored outside. However, the HOA’s enforcement power is not unlimited. Associations can impose fines or other penalties only if their rules align with both Florida’s HOA statute (Chapter 720, Florida Statutes) and local government regulations.

Recent state law changes have clarified that associations cannot adopt or enforce parking rules that unreasonably restrict the normal use of private property. This includes blanket bans on certain vehicle types unless the restriction is explicitly authorized by the recorded declaration. Boards must now follow formal notice and hearing procedures before imposing fines or towing vehicles, ensuring greater due process for homeowners.

Municipal Code and Local Enforcement

The City of Palm Coast also enforces its own parking ordinances, which take precedence over HOA rules in certain areas. City code prohibits vehicles from blocking sidewalks, parking on lawns, or obstructing public rights-of-way. It also regulates street parking to maintain access for emergency vehicles and sanitation services. HOAs may supplement these requirements but cannot override city authority.

Common HOA Rules Still in Place

Many Palm Coast HOA communities continue to require residents to park overnight in their driveways or garages, rather than on the street. It’s also common to see prohibitions on storing boats, trailers, or recreational vehicles in view of the street, as these are often considered aesthetic concerns tied to property values. Some associations have added flexibility for short-term guest parking or temporary permits, reflecting a broader move toward more reasonable enforcement standards.

Evolving Landscape for Homeowners

As state and local rules evolve, Palm Coast homeowners should review their HOA’s covenants and the city’s current parking ordinances before assuming what is or isn’t enforceable. Boards are encouraged to consult legal counsel before enforcing long-standing vehicle restrictions, especially where new state law limits their authority over private driveways.

These passes are tied to specific time limits and vehicle information to prevent misuse or unauthorized long-term parking. Many associations now use electronic registration systems or smartphone apps to streamline this process. Residents can log into an online portal to request guest passes, while management staff can monitor occupancy and expiration times in real time.

Condominium boards, acting under the authority of Chapter 718, Florida Statutes, and the community’s governing documents, may enforce parking rules through warnings, fines, or towing. Enforcement typically depends on whether proper tow signage has been posted as required under §715.07, Florida Statutes. These signs must clearly identify the towing company, contact number, and notice that unauthorized vehicles are subject to removal at the owner’s expense.

Association rulebooks and posted signage often contain explicit language such as:

“Guest vehicles without passes may be towed.”

This direct approach underscores how critical compliance is to maintaining order and fairness within limited parking spaces. Consistent enforcement also protects associations from claims of selective treatment or negligence in managing common elements.

Management’s Role

Property management companies and community association managers play a key role in administering the guest-pass system. They issue passes, maintain parking logs, and coordinate with towing vendors when violations occur. Clear communication and documentation—often through posted notices, newsletters, or rule PDFs—ensure residents understand the procedures and consequences of non-compliance.

In densely populated condominium communities, guest parking control is more than a courtesy—it’s an operational necessity. The guest-pass system represents a structured, legally compliant way to manage shared resources, uphold community standards, and ensure that both residents and visitors follow the same fair rules.

The HOA model in Palm Coast emphasizes cooperation between associations, homeowners, and city enforcement. D. Towing: legal requirements and practical steps

  • Florida’s Towing Law: What Associations and Property Owners Must Know Before Removing Vehicles

Towing from private property in Florida is governed by strict statutory requirements designed to protect both vehicle owners and property managers from unfair or unlawful practices. Whether the property is managed by a condominium association, homeowners’ association (HOA), or an individual owner, the state mandates clear notice and signage before any vehicle can be legally towed. These requirements are outlined in Chapter 715, Florida Statutes, which establishes the state’s towing and storage standards.

Signage and Notice Requirements

Before any towing can occur, conspicuous “tow-away zone” signage must be properly posted on the property. Florida law specifies not only where and how the signs must be displayed, but also what information they must include. Signs must:

  • Clearly state that unauthorized vehicles will be towed at the owner’s expense.

  • Include the name and telephone number of the towing company responsible for removals.

  • Meet the minimum size and reflectivity standards set by statute to ensure visibility both day and night.

  • Be posted at each property entrance or at other prominent locations, depending on the site layout.

These signage requirements apply equally to residential associations, apartment complexes, shopping centers, and other private properties that enforce parking rules.

Statutory Compliance for Associations

Community associations—both condominiums under Chapter 718 and HOAs under Chapter 720—must comply fully with Chapter 715 before authorizing a tow. Even if association rules or declarations provide for towing, those provisions are enforceable only when the statutory prerequisites are met.

For example, a condominium association that tows a vehicle from a common parking area without proper signage or without contracting a licensed towing company could face legal exposure. The same applies to HOAs that attempt to remove vehicles from common lots or private driveways without adhering to state notice standards.

Liability for Noncompliance

Failure to follow Chapter 715 can result in significant civil liability for both the property owner (or association) and the towing company. Vehicle owners may bring claims for unlawful towing, seek reimbursement for damages, and recover attorney’s fees if the tow is found to violate statutory requirements. As a result, associations and property managers are advised to review their towing contracts, signage placement, and recordkeeping practices regularly to ensure compliance.

Best Practices for Property Managers and Associations

  • Conduct a signage audit at least once a year.

  • Keep copies of tow contracts and posted signage for recordkeeping.

  • Use only licensed towing vendors familiar with Florida’s statutory notice rules.

  • Provide clear communication to residents about parking and towing procedures through newsletters, rulebooks, or community websites.

Florida’s towing laws establish a clear balance between private property rights and consumer protection. Associations and property owners that follow Chapter 715’s signage and notice standards can lawfully enforce parking rules, while those that don’t risk costly disputes and liability. Proper compliance isn’t just a legal formality—it’s an essential safeguard for fair and transparent community management.

Homeowners’ associations (HOAs) in Palm Coast and throughout Florida must navigate a more complex legal landscape than condominiums when it comes to towing and parking enforcement. Because single-family homes generally include privately owned driveways, HOA boards must distinguish clearly between what can be enforced on association-controlled common property and what remains under homeowner control.

Without proper signage, towing is not legally permitted.

HOAs typically formalize this process through a management contract or enforcement resolution, specifying when a tow may be ordered and who has the authority to request it—usually the property manager or board president.

Towing from private driveways, however, is far more legally sensitive. Florida law distinguishes between towing from commercial properties or condominium common elements—where ownership and enforcement are collective—and towing from individually owned single-family lots.

Because the homeowner’s driveway is deeded private property, an HOA’s ability to order a tow depends entirely on the recorded covenants and the specific consent of the owner. Unauthorized towing from private residential property can expose both the association and the towing company to liability for trespass or conversion. Palm Coast’s municipal ordinances also play a key role in defining what parking rules can be enforced. City code regulates the parking of commercial vehicles, RVs, and boats in residential neighborhoods, often prohibiting long-term outdoor storage or vehicles that obstruct sidewalks and rights-of-way.

In recent years, Palm Coast has seen ongoing local discussion about updating commercial vehicle and driveway parking regulations, with residents and HOAs debating how to balance property aesthetics, homeowner freedom, and neighborhood consistency. These evolving city-level policies directly influence how HOAs enforce—or limit—their own towing and parking procedures.

For Palm Coast HOAs, implementing towing rules requires a careful blend of statutory compliance, local coordination, and respect for homeowner property rights. While associations can manage and tow vehicles from common areas with proper signage and contracts, towing from private driveways remains a legal gray zone that demands caution. As local regulations evolve, HOAs must adapt their enforcement strategies to remain both lawful and community-minded.


E. Fines, notice, and homeowners’ rights to contest

  • Notice and Process: How Florida Associations Must Follow Proper Procedures Before Enforcing Fines or Towing

In Florida, community associations—both condominiums and homeowners’ associations (HOAs)—must follow clear procedural steps before taking enforcement actions such as imposing fines, towing vehicles, or filing liens. These procedures ensure fairness, transparency, and compliance with state law, protecting both the rights of residents and the authority of the association.

Notice and Hearings Before Fines

Before an association can levy a fine for a rule violation, it must adhere to the procedural safeguards outlined in its governing documents and Florida law. Under Chapter 718 (for condominiums) and Chapter 720 (for homeowners’ associations) of the Florida Statutes, due process is mandatory.

This process typically includes:

  1. Written Notice — The association must notify the alleged violator in writing, specifying the nature of the violation and the proposed fine or corrective action.

  2. Right to a Hearing — The homeowner or resident must be given the opportunity to appear before an impartial committee—often called a fining or grievance committee—to present their side.

  3. Board Action — The board may impose the fine only after the committee confirms that the violation occurred and approves the penalty.

Failure to provide proper notice or conduct a hearing can invalidate the fine and expose the association to disputes or legal challenges.

Towing and Public Notice Requirements

For towing, Florida law imposes an entirely separate form of notice—statutory notice to the public. Under Section 715.07, Florida Statutes, no vehicle may be towed from private property unless conspicuous tow-away zone signage is properly posted. These signs must:

  • Clearly state that unauthorized vehicles will be towed at the owner’s expense;

  • Include the name and phone number of the authorized towing company; and

  • Meet statutory size, visibility, and placement standards.

This signage serves as the public’s notice of enforcement authority. Without it, even if a vehicle violates association rules, towing could be deemed unlawful.

Liens and Collection Actions

If fines remain unpaid and escalate into lien or foreclosure actions, the association must again follow the internal procedures established by its bylaws and the relevant chapter of the Florida Statutes. This includes providing proper notice of intent to record a lien, allowing time for the homeowner to cure the violation or pay the fine, and following the statutory timeline before proceeding with further collection efforts.

Why Process Matters

The purpose of these procedural safeguards is to ensure that enforcement actions are consistent, transparent, and defensible. Proper notice and fair hearings protect residents’ rights while shielding associations from liability or claims of selective enforcement. A board that follows its bylaws and statutory requirements strengthens community trust and minimizes costly legal disputes.

Florida law makes it clear: associations cannot act arbitrarily when imposing fines, towing vehicles, or pursuing liens. They must comply with both internal governing documents and state statutes—including the notice, hearing, and signage requirements set forth in Chapters 718, 720, and 715. By adhering to these standards, associations maintain lawful authority and community fairness in every enforcement decision.


  • Contesting a Tow or Fine: What Florida Residents Can Do When Association Enforcement Goes Too Far

Residents living in Florida condominiums and homeowners’ associations (HOAs) are subject to community rules that govern everything from parking to property maintenance. But when an association or towing company takes enforcement action—such as towing a vehicle or imposing a fine—homeowners still have rights. Florida law provides clear procedures for disputing these actions and ensuring that associations and their contractors follow proper legal and procedural standards.

Contesting a Tow

If your vehicle has been towed from private property governed by a condominium or HOA, Florida law gives you specific rights under Section 715.07, Florida Statutes. The towing company and property owner must meet all statutory requirements before the tow, including proper signage and lawful authorization.

You have the right to demand detailed information immediately after your vehicle is removed. This includes:

  • The name, address, and telephone number of the towing company;

  • The storage location where your vehicle is being held; and

  • The charges associated with towing and storage.

If this information is not provided, or if proper “tow-away zone” signage was not posted at every entrance or in a conspicuous location, you may have grounds to contest the tow. Vehicle owners can file a complaint with local law enforcement or pursue civil remedies if the tow violated state law. In some cases, a wrongful or improperly authorized tow can entitle the vehicle owner to damages and reimbursement of fees.

Challenging Association Fines

For association-imposed fines, the process is different. Both condominiums (Chapter 718, Florida Statutes) and homeowners’ associations (Chapter 720, Florida Statutes) are required to provide notice and an opportunity for a hearing before a fine becomes final.

If you receive a notice of fine or rule violation:

  1. Review the notice carefully. It should specify the alleged violation, the amount of the fine, and your right to contest it.

  2. Request a hearing. You have the right to appear before an impartial committee—usually made up of non-board community members—to present your side and evidence.

  3. Ask for documentation. Request a copy of the governing rule or covenant being cited and any photos or reports used as evidence.

  4. Follow the appeal process. If the fine is upheld and you believe due process was not followed, you can seek review by an attorney or pursue mediation or arbitration depending on the type of community.

When to Seek Legal Help

If you believe a tow or fine was imposed without proper notice, hearing, or signage—or if the association fails to follow its own governing documents—it may be time to consult an attorney experienced in Florida HOA or condominium law. Legal counsel can review the association’s actions for compliance with Chapters 715, 718, or 720 of the Florida Statutes and help you pursue a formal challenge or resolution.

Both towing and fines are legitimate enforcement tools when used properly—but they must comply with Florida’s statutory requirements and the association’s own procedures. As a homeowner or resident, you have the right to contest improper actions, request documentation, and insist on fair treatment under the law. Understanding your rights and the proper process can help you resolve disputes effectively and protect your property from unlawful enforcement practices.

Contesting a Tow

If your vehicle has been towed from a condominium or HOA community, Florida Statute §715.07 governs how that process must occur. The law requires towing companies and property owners to meet strict standards before and after removing a vehicle.

You have the right to request and receive detailed information immediately, including:

  • The name, address, and phone number of the towing company;

  • The storage location of your vehicle; and

  • The total charges for towing and storage.

If proper tow-away zone signage was not clearly posted—or if the towing company failed to follow the procedural rules under Chapter 715—you may have grounds to contest the tow. Contesting an Association Fine

  1. Written Notice — The association must send a formal notice describing the alleged violation and proposed fine.

  2. Right to a Hearing — The homeowner or resident has the right to a fair hearing before an impartial committee of non-board members.

If you believe the fine is unjustified, you should use the association’s internal appeal or hearing process first. Document all communications and request copies of relevant rules, evidence, or meeting minutes.

When to Seek Legal Assistance

If the association or towing company fails to follow statutory or procedural requirements, it’s wise to consult an attorney experienced in Florida condominium and HOA law. Legal counsel can help determine whether due process was violated and guide you in filing a complaint, mediation, or court challenge.

Whether you’re dealing with a towed vehicle or a disputed fine, Florida law ensures you have the right to information, notice, and fair process. Associations and towing companies must comply with both statutory notice rules and their own governing documents. By asserting your rights and following proper procedures, you can effectively contest unfair enforcement actions while protecting your property and peace of mind.

This autonomy, however, is not absolute. HOA covenants and city ordinances can still restrict how that space is used, especially when it comes to the types of vehicles and overnight parking.

These rules often prohibit the parking of commercial vehicles, boats, RVs, trailers, or oversized trucks overnight — even in a private driveway. The intent is to preserve neighborhood aesthetics and property values. While homeowners may consider their driveways private property, these restrictions form part of the deed agreement and are legally enforceable by the HOA board.

Local council actions can also impact enforcement. Palm Coast’s city ordinances occasionally intersect with HOA rules, particularly regarding street parking, overnight storage, and code enforcement. For example, even if an HOA is lenient, a city ordinance may prohibit certain vehicles from parking in visible areas or enforce time limits on street parking. Conversely, if the city eases regulations, an HOA may still enforce its own stricter rules through fines or architectural review provisions.

For homeowners, the key takeaway is to read their HOA’s covenants closely and stay informed about any city-level changes. Council amendments or enforcement adjustments can affect what’s permissible, even on what feels like private space. When in doubt, confirm both with your HOA management and the City of Palm Coast’s code enforcement department to avoid unexpected violations.


  • Signage and Towing Risk: How Enforcement Differs Between Condos and HOAs

Both condominium communities and homeowners associations (HOAs) rely on clearly posted tow-away signs to make vehicle towing lawful under Florida law. However, the frequency and formality of towing enforcement differ significantly between these two settings.

In condominium complexes—especially those with shared parking areas defined as common elements—towing enforcement tends to be more systematic. Condos often operate under a permit or decal regime, where each resident and guest vehicle must display an approved sticker or temporary pass. Vehicles parked without proper identification can be towed swiftly, sometimes within hours, under the authority of the condominium board or property manager. These practices are permitted as long as signage meets the statutory requirements: visible tow-away warnings, the name and phone number of the towing company, and clear identification of the area subject to enforcement.

By contrast, single-family HOAs generally exercise towing power less frequently, since homeowners typically control their driveways and have more direct responsibility for parking. Towing in HOA neighborhoods usually applies to shared streets, guest lots, or clubhouse parking areas—and even then, it’s less common unless chronic violations occur. Nonetheless, both condos and HOAs must comply with the same legal framework under Florida Statutes §715.07, which mandates proper signage and procedural notice before towing can occur.

Residents and guests should always look for posted tow-away signs at entrances and parking areas before leaving a vehicle unattended. In condos, the risk of towing is substantially higher, as management actively monitors decals and visitor passes to ensure compliance with community regulations.


  • Enforcement Culture: How Condos and HOAs Differ in Parking Oversight

The culture of parking enforcement varies sharply between condominium associations and homeowners associations (HOAs) across Florida. While both are governed by community covenants and state statutes, their operational structures lead to very different approaches in how rules are applied and enforced.

Condominium boards tend to operate with centralized, day-to-day parking controls. Many condo communities have on-site management offices, staffed gate booths, and formal decal or permit systems that track resident and guest vehicles in real time. These measures allow condo boards to monitor compliance continuously—making enforcement more immediate and visible. Residents without proper decals, expired permits, or vehicles parked in restricted zones can be cited or towed promptly under the authority of the condominium association.

In contrast, HOAs typically rely on covenant enforcement and periodic compliance reviews rather than daily monitoring. Because homeowners in these communities usually control their own driveways and private lots, HOA boards focus on enforcing broader appearance or use standards—such as prohibiting commercial vehicles, boats, or trailers visible from the street. Violations are generally handled through written notices, fines, or architectural review committee (ARC) actions rather than instant towing or ticketing. Enforcement often occurs during scheduled inspections or after a neighbor complaint rather than through continuous surveillance.

These contrasting cultures reflect the underlying design of each community type: condos function more like multi-unit properties with shared infrastructure, while HOAs resemble neighborhoods of individual homes governed by collective rules. Understanding this distinction helps residents anticipate how strictly parking rules will be applied—and by whom—before moving into or managing either type of community.


G. Best practices — what homeowners and guests should do

  • Always keep a copy/photo of your association’s parking rules and your vehicle registration on file with management.

  • Request and display guest passes where required. If you’re hosting long-term guests, notify management in advance.

  • If your car is towed, immediately ask the towing firm for the statutory paperwork and the reason; verify signage was posted at the property entrance. If procedures weren’t followed, document photos and ask the association for its towing contract.

  • Boards should document parking rules clearly, publish a short “parking cheat sheet” for owners, and ensure signs meet Florida’s tow-sign statute. Boards should also keep a consistent guest-pass system to avoid arbitrary enforcement.
